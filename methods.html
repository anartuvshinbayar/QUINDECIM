How Quindecim works

Quindecim answers one question for each of Mongolia's 337 soums and districts: is the climate here really changing, or is it just a few unusual years? This page explains the data, the tests and their limits, and links every result, so anyone can check our work.

The data

One value per area per year, for five questions. Only complete seasons are used: the newest year, 2026, is the full June to August 2026 for the summer questions and December 2025 to February 2026 for winter, and a frost year counts only once its frost has come.

Greenness is measured over grassland only. A whole-soum average would mix in desert, water, forest, fields and towns. We keep the 1 km pixels that MODIS land cover (MCD12Q1, class 10 "Grasslands") calls grassland in most years since 2001, and use the same pixels every year, so a trend can never come from a change in which land is measured. Composites flagged as snow or cloud are dropped. Four areas have under 10 km² of grassland and get no greenness value.

Why these seasons. Summer rain and greenness decide the pasture that herds live on; winter temperature matters for dzud, the harsh winters that kill livestock; the first autumn frost ends the growing season.

The 337 areas

Mongolia officially has 330 soums and Ulaanbaatar has 9 districts, 339 in all. We use the FAO GAUL 2025 boundaries, the standard global set of administrative areas, which draw 328 soums and Ulaanbaatar's 9 districts: 337 areas. On the site they are all called soums. An aimag's result uses the average of its soums for each year (years where at least half of them have a value); Ulaanbaatar is counted with the 21 aimags, making 22.

How a trend is judged

The same rules apply to every area and every question.

  1. At least 20 yearly values, otherwise the site says there is not enough data. One value per year means there is no seasonal cycle to remove.
  2. Mann-Kendall test for the direction and the p value: it compares every year with every later year and counts ups against downs, so one extreme year cannot make a trend.
  3. Correction for year-to-year correlation (Hamed and Rao 1998). A dry year is often followed by another, and such runs make the plain test find too many trends. The correction widens the test's uncertainty when the years are correlated; it is only ever used to make the test stricter.
  4. Check for chance across all areas. With 337 areas, about 17 would pass p < 0.05 by luck alone. Every area also gets a Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate q value among all areas (aimags among all aimags) for the same question and years.
  5. Verdict. A real trend if q < 0.05 (and p < 0.05); a possible trend if p < 0.10 but not real; otherwise not proven. "Not proven" is a real answer: the change over these years is no bigger than the usual ups and downs.
  6. Rate. Sen's slope (the middle of all year-to-year slopes) gives the rate per decade. A straight-line fit with a t-test and a 95% confidence interval, using an effective sample size for correlated years (Santer et al. 2000), gives a second opinion; if the two disagree, the site says so.
  7. In plain words. Every result starts with one sentence: the change along the trend line from the first year to the last, and whether it could be chance.
  8. On the map, the colour depth is the size of the change and the fill is how sure it is: solid for a real trend, striped for a possible one, pale for not proven. A large change that could be chance never shows as a strong colour.

Checked. All statistics run in the browser (js/stats.js, no libraries). A test script runs 15 series (ties, an extreme year, strongly correlated years, alternating years, a gap) through it and through independent Python code (SciPy, and pymannkendall where installed); they agree to within 0.0000001.

Each result states its own period. Records differ in length (greenness from 2001, the others from the early 1980s), so a 26-year and a 45-year trend are not directly comparable.

Checked against weather stations

A land model is only as good as its match with what thermometers measure. We compared FLDAS with ten weather stations across Mongolia (NOAA GHCN-Daily), each against the area it stands in, using the same seasons (a season counts when at least 80 of its days have a value).

Summer: FLDAS rises and falls with the stations year by year (correlation 0.73 to 0.96, median 0.93) and, like them, warms at 10 of 10; but it warms less at 10 of 10 (FLDAS +0.2 to +0.5 °C per decade, stations +0.5 to +0.8), so the site's summer warming figures are cautious. Winter: FLDAS reads milder than the stations, by up to 12.6 °C in the cold western basins where winter inversions trap cold air (median +3.2 °C); FLDAS shows a clear winter trend at 0 of 10 stations and the stations themselves at 2, which matches the site's "not proven" for winters.

Limits

  • Straight-line trends only: a change that sped up or reversed is summarised as one rate.
  • Neighbouring areas share weather, and often share the same model cell (FLDAS about 11 × 8 km, POWER about 55 × 47 km), so their results are not independent. The corrections make the verdicts stricter, not perfect.
  • Rainfall from a land model is the least certain of the five questions; read rainfall trends with care.
  • Winter temperatures from FLDAS differ from weather stations in cold basins, where winter inversions trap cold air (see the station check above). Trends are more reliable than absolute winter values.
  • The first frost uses one coarse grid cell at the soum centre: good for the general timing, not for the exact frost day in one valley.
  • A trend is not a cause.
  • The Mongolian text and soum names are being checked by a native speaker.
